# Case 7 – Calcinosis in systemic sclerosis (final English version for the PANLAR 2027 form)

**Title:** What the skin hides: two clinical-radiological extremes of calcinosis in systemic sclerosis

**Topic:** 7. Imaging in Rheumatology

**Keywords:** Imaging · Skin · Descriptive Studies

### Background/Objective

Calcinosis is a frequent manifestation of systemic sclerosis (SSc), yet its true burden is often underestimated: physical examination detects only superficial deposits, whereas radiography reveals their real extent and associated lesions. It causes pain, ulceration, infection and disability, and no treatment has proven effective in controlled clinical trials. Objective: to illustrate, through clinical-radiological correlation, the two extremes of the calcinosis spectrum in SSc: the acral and the massive soft-tissue forms.

### Methods

Retrospective review of the medical records, clinical photographs, radiographs and laboratory studies of two patients with SSc and calcinosis followed by rheumatology. Location, distribution, radiological pattern, associated lesions and visible complications were described.

### Results

Both patients had normal serum calcium and phosphate, consistent with dystrophic calcinosis. Patient 1: a 48-year-old woman with anticentromere-positive SSc (ANA 1:640, centromere pattern; anti-Scl-70 negative), sclerodactyly and microstomia. Pelvic radiography revealed massive, bilateral and symmetric calcinosis, with confluent plaque-like deposits and tumor-like conglomerates in the gluteal and trochanteric regions and the lateral thighs (Figure 1A), far more extensive than the visible lesions suggested. On the surface, it presented as painful subcutaneous nodules on the flank and buttocks, several ulcerated and draining chalky white material, with scars from previous surgical removals (Figure 1B). Patient 2: a 41-year-old woman with SSc (modified Rodnan skin score 18), Raynaud's phenomenon, prior digital ulcers, microstomia and Sjögren's syndrome. She had clustered punctate white deposits on several fingertips, with flexion contractures (Figure 2A). Hand radiographs showed multiple bilateral soft-tissue calcifications in the fingertips, most prominent in the left thumb, left middle finger and right middle finger, coexisting with acro-osteolysis (Figure 2B).

### Conclusion

Calcinosis in SSc is not a single entity: it ranges from punctate acral deposits to massive conglomerates in the gluteal region and thighs. The skin shows only a fraction; radiography, widely available and inexpensive, reveals the true extent and associated lesions, such as acro-osteolysis, which point to the underlying digital vasculopathy. Incorporating imaging into routine assessment helps gauge disease burden and anticipate complications such as ulceration and infection.
